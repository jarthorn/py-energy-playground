PROJECTION: INCREMENTAL ONTARIO WINTER PEAK (CAPACITY) IF ALL HOUSEHOLDS SWITCH
TO HEAT PUMPS BY 2050
===============================================================================

Companion to the annual-ENERGY analysis (heatpump-2050-projection*). This one
estimates the incremental WINTER PEAK electricity demand (GW) from converting all
Ontario residential natural-gas space + water heating to cold-climate heat pumps.

Per request, 2050 household scaling is FIXED at 1.26x, and the spread is taken
over a TEMPERATURE / weather basis: the 10-year average, minimum, and maximum of
Ontario residential gas consumption. Two heat-pump COP variants are reported:
  * seasonal COP 3.5  -- seasonal-average efficiency (optimistic at the peak hour)
  * cold-day COP 2.0  -- assumed 2050 efficiency at the winter design temperature,
                         reflecting progress over current cold-climate units
                         (which are nearer ~1.5 on the coldest days)

Headline result (incremental winter peak added, over the 6.3% baseline):
                         seasonal COP 3.5     cold-day COP 2.0
  10-yr minimum gas year:    ~7.2 GW              ~12.6 GW
  10-yr average gas year:    ~8.0 GW              ~14.0 GW
  10-yr maximum gas year:    ~8.7 GW              ~15.2 GW

For context, the IESO reference 2050 WINTER peak is ~35.1 GW. So this adds roughly
20-25% on top of the reference winter peak at seasonal COP, rising to ~36-43% at
the more realistic cold-day COP 2.0 (the IESO high-demand 2050 winter peak is
~41.8 GW -- i.e. the cold-day variant alone is comparable to the entire
reference-to-high-demand gap).

Output table: heatpump-2050-winter-peak.csv
Model script: bin/heatpump_peak_projection.py


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
METHOD (degree-day / design-temperature)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Only SPACE heating is weather-driven and sets the peak. Its annual energy is
proportional to heating degree-days; its peak power occurs at the winter design
(coldest) temperature:

    E_space  = U_agg * HDD18 * 24            (U_agg = aggregate GW per degC)
    P_space  = U_agg * (T_indoor - T_design)
    =>  P_space[GW] = E_space[GWh] * (T_indoor - T_design) / (HDD18 * 24)

Electricity peak = P_space / COP.

WATER heating is treated as weather-independent and contributes ~its average
electric power at the system peak (water_peak_factor = 1.0).

Total incremental peak = (space + water electric peak) x (1 - 0.063 baseline).


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INPUTS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Gas basis (StatCan 25-10-0059, Ontario residential, from
ontario-residential-natgas-by-year.csv), TWh thermal/yr:
    10-yr average = 87.7   |  minimum = 79.2 (2024)  |  maximum = 95.5 (2019)
  scaled x1.26 to 2050 households -> 110.5 / 99.8 / 120.4 TWh.

Heating split / efficiencies (same as energy model):
    space 70% @ AFUE 0.85 ;  water 30% @ EF 0.63.

Climate (Ontario, population-weighted):
    HDD18 = 3700  (Env. Canada normals: Toronto 3434, Ottawa 4477)
    T_indoor / balance = 18 degC  (consistent with HDD base)
    T_design = -19 degC  (Ontario Building Code SB-1 Jan 2.5%: Toronto -18,
                          Ottawa -24, population-weighted ~ -19)
    => design delta-T = 37 degC; implied ~2370 effective full-load heating hours.

Heat-pump COP variants = 3.5 (seasonal) and 2.0 (cold-day at design temperature).
Baseline = 6.3% households already on HPs.
Context peak (IESO reference 2050 winter peak) = 35.1 GW.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHY TWO COP VARIANTS (cold-weather efficiency risk)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The seasonal COP of 3.5 is a YEAR-AVERAGE value; the winter system peak occurs on
the coldest hour, when a heat pump's instantaneous COP is much lower. The cold-day
COP 2.0 variant captures this: it assumes the 2050 fleet performs at COP ~2.0 at
the -19 degC design temperature -- an improvement over today's cold-climate units
(nearer ~1.5 on the coldest days). The cold-day variant roughly DOUBLES the peak
impact (to ~14 GW on the average basis).

Even COP 2.0 may be optimistic at the extreme: below their balance point, heat
pumps commonly fall back to electric RESISTANCE (COP 1.0). For reference, at
resistance the space-heating peak alone would be ~27.4 GW (10-yr average basis).
A full peak/capacity study should use a temperature-dependent COP curve with a
resistance-backup balance point against Ontario's coldest design hour; the COP 2.0
figures here are a reasonable mid-point, not a floor.

Other caveats:
- Population-weighted single-zone climate (no zonal/coldest-region detail).
- No demand response, thermal storage, hybrid (gas-backup) heat pumps, or
  building-envelope improvements -- all of which would reduce the peak.
- Water-heating peak coincidence assumed ~average (factor 1.0); storage HPWHs
  buffer demand, but a higher coincidence factor would raise the total modestly.
- Energy-model space:water split and gas efficiencies carried over unchanged.
